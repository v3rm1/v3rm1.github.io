From Legg-Hutter to ARC: What Are We Actually Optimising?

Moving beyond next-token compression toward algorithmic information theory, skill acquisition efficiency, and structured search across latent manifolds.

Author

NoteIn brief

Large language models get better mostly by absorbing more data, which makes them very good at problems that resemble what they have already seen. The formal theories of intelligence discussed here measure something different: how efficiently a system learns new skills from little experience. This post walks through two such theories (Legg-Hutter universal intelligence and Chollet’s ARC) and argues that the recent shift towards search and verification at inference time moves practice closer to them.

1 The Parametric Illusion

The contemporary machine learning discourse conflates two fundamentally divergent constructs: crystallised competence and general intelligence.

By training multi-billion parameter autoregressive models on petabytes of human text, the frontier artificial intelligence industry has engineered extraordinary cultural retrieval engines. When a large language model correctly solves a differential equation or synthesises a Python script, its behaviour is well described as interpolation over patterns learnt from trillions of observed human tokens. When it faces genuinely novel problems far from its pre-training distribution, its performance often degrades sharply. The system lacks a mechanism for genuine hypothesis revision outside its static weights.

To elucidate why parametric scaling alone cannot yield Artificial General Intelligence, we must return to foundational principles: algorithmic information theory, formal definitions of universal intelligence, and the geometry of inductive search.

2 The Legg-Hutter Universal Intelligence Formalism

In 2007, Shane Legg and Marcus Hutter proposed an influential, non-anthropocentric formal measure of general intelligence (Legg and Hutter 2007), combining Ray Solomonoff’s algorithmic probability (Solomonoff 1964) with sequential decision theory.

2.1 Mathematical Formulation

Let \(E\) denote the set of computable stochastic environments whose total reward is bounded, \(\sum_{t=1}^\infty r_t \le 1\). Let \(\pi\) represent an agent policy interacting with environment \(\mu \in E\) via alternating sequences of actions \(a_t \in \mathcal{A}\), observations \(o_t \in \mathcal{O}\), and rewards \(r_t \ge 0\).

The Universal Intelligence \(\Upsilon(\pi)\) of an agent policy \(\pi\) is formally defined as:

\[\Upsilon(\pi) \triangleq \sum_{\mu \in E} 2^{-K(\mu)} V_\mu^\pi\]

where:

  • \(V_\mu^\pi \triangleq \mathbb{E}_\mu^\pi \left[ \sum_{t=1}^\infty r_t \right]\) is the expected total reward achieved by policy \(\pi\) in environment \(\mu\). Because rewards in \(E\) are bounded, the sum converges without a discount factor; Legg and Hutter deliberately avoid one, so that the measure does not fix an arbitrary planning horizon.
  • \(K(\mu)\) denotes the prefix Kolmogorov complexity of environment \(\mu\) (Li and Vitányi 2008)—specifically, the length in bits of the shortest self-delimiting binary program that simulates the transition dynamics of \(\mu\) on a reference universal prefix Turing machine \(\mathcal{U}\).
  • \(2^{-K(\mu)}\) operationalises algorithmic probability (the mathematical formulation of Occam’s razor), assigning exponentially superior a priori weight to compact, lawful causal systems while attenuating ad-hoc, convoluted worlds.

2.2 Epistemic Implications

  1. Domain Invariance: The agent is not evaluated against an arbitrary empirical benchmark (such as chess, standardised academic examinations, or programming leaderboards), but across the space of all computable environments.
  2. The Occam Inductive Bias: An agent cannot attain high universal intelligence through exhaustive task-specific memorisation. To maximise \(\Upsilon(\pi)\), the agent must excel across fundamentally lawful environments while maintaining robustness in complex stochastic settings.
  3. The AIXI Horizon: The theoretical agent that maximises \(\Upsilon(\pi)\) is AIXI (Hutter 2005), which combines Solomonoff induction with expectimax planning up to a horizon \(m\):

\[a_t^* \triangleq \arg\max_{a_t} \sum_{o_t r_t} \cdots \max_{a_m} \sum_{o_m r_m} \left[ r_t + \cdots + r_m \right] \sum_{q \,:\, \mathcal{U}(q,\, a_1 \ldots a_m) = o_1 r_1 \ldots o_m r_m} 2^{-\ell(q)}\]

where \(\mathcal{U}\) is a universal prefix Turing machine, the inner sum runs over every program \(q\) that, given the actions \(a_1 \ldots a_m\), outputs the percepts \(o_1 r_1 \ldots o_m r_m\), and \(\ell(q)\) is the length of \(q\) in bits.

Prefix Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable (computing it would solve the halting problem), and AIXI is not even limit-computable, so it cannot be approximated arbitrarily well with finite computation (Leike and Hutter 2015). It is best read as a theoretical reference point for bounded agents rather than a blueprint.

3 Chollet’s ARC: Intelligence as Skill Acquisition Efficiency

While Legg and Hutter supply the uncomputable theoretical bound, François Chollet (2019) translated algorithmic information theory into a falsifiable, finite measurement framework: the Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus (ARC).

3.1 Deconstructing Crystallised Competence

Chollet delineates a vital dichotomy between crystallised competence and fluid intelligence:

  • Crystallised Competence: Static execution of learned behaviour over familiar distributions. A specialised system mastering Go or chess exhibits immense capability, yet zero fluid intelligence if it cannot generalise when the rules undergo minor topological perturbation.
  • Fluid Intelligence: The capacity to synthesise novel behavioural programs in response to unfamiliar environments using minimal experience.

3.2 The Measure of Skill Acquisition Efficiency

Formally, Chollet defines intelligence not as skill at any one task, but as skill-acquisition efficiency: how much generalisation a system achieves relative to the prior knowledge and experience it needed. Stripped of its weighting terms, his definition averages over a scope of tasks \(T\):

\[\text{Intelligence} \propto \operatorname*{Avg}_{T} \left[ \frac{\text{GD}_T}{P_T + E_T} \right]\]

where \(\text{GD}_T\) is the generalisation difficulty of reaching a threshold skill on task \(T\), and \(P_T\) and \(E_T\) are the priors and experience the system used, all measured in bits of algorithmic information. The full definition also weights each task by its value and averages over learning curricula. Note that priors and experience are added: a system can only be credited for generalisation it did not get for free.

In ARC, tasks are deliberately curated to eliminate linguistic and cultural retrieval shortcuts:

  • Tasks demand inductive inference over compact discrete 2D grid transformations.
  • Solutions require synthesising short latent programs grounded exclusively in human-innate Core Knowledge Priors (Spelke and Kinzler 2007):
    • Objectness (cohesion, persistence, boundaries).
    • Goal-directed agency (intentional state modifications).
    • Elementary geometry and topology (symmetry, translation, containment).
    • Basic arithmetic (counting, parity, ordering).
  • Because test tasks present previously unencountered transformation rules, high performance cannot be secured via memorised parametric interpolation across pre-training corpora.

4 Intelligence as a Multi-Tier Search Ontology

If intelligence cannot be reduced to static token interpolation, and if uncomputable Solomonoff induction cannot be executed directly on silicon, how do physical agents realise adaptive capability?

My working hypothesis is that intelligence is best understood as structured search across hierarchical representations. Rather than exploring unconstrained state spaces exhaustively, capable agents compress the search problem at four levels:

4.1 The Four-Tier Search Hierarchy

Tier Conceptual Role Mathematical Mechanism Operational Instantiation
Tier 1: Direct State Navigation Environmental interaction Traversal of state transitions \((s_t \xrightarrow{a_t} s_{t+1})\) Execution of primitive motor or linguistic actions
Tier 2: Heuristic & Value Surfaces Pruning combinatorial branching Scalar potential surfaces \(V(s), Q(s, a)\) steering local search Critic networks, Bellman value estimators
Tier 3: State-Abstraction Manifolds Geometric volume contraction Factoring symmetries \(\mathcal{S}_{\text{comp}} = \mathcal{S}_{\text{raw}} / \mathcal{G}_{\text{symmetry}}\) Group-equivariant representations, latent world models
Tier 4: Meta-Search & Program Synthesis Algorithm discovery Searching over the space of search algorithms \(\pi_{\text{search}} \in \Pi\) Test-time tree search, Monte Carlo Tree Search, self-refining verification

At Tier 3, discovering invariance under translation, rotation or permutation shrinks the effective state space: quotienting by a symmetry group \(\mathcal{G}\) that acts freely divides the number of distinct states by \(|\mathcal{G}|\). At Tier 4, the architecture avoids static heuristics entirely, actively generating and validating verification routines at inference time.

4.2 A Thermodynamic Analogy

There is a suggestive analogy between compressing uncertainty in search and thermodynamics. Landauer’s principle (Landauer 1961) states that erasing information has an unavoidable energy cost: erasing \(N\) bits at temperature \(T\) dissipates at least

\[Q \ge N \, k_B T \ln 2\]

of heat into the environment. Seen this way, an agent that narrows a wide space of hypotheses down to a compact causal model is a physical system paying, at least in principle, an energy cost for discarding the alternatives. In practice this bound is far below the energy that real hardware uses, so it is a conceptual lens rather than a practical constraint.

5 The Architectural Phase Transition: Test-Time Compute

There are growing signs that pre-training scaling alone is running into limits. The stock of high-quality human-generated text is finite, and projections suggest frontier training runs will exhaust it within years (Villalobos et al. 2024).

The emerging paradigm of inference-time reasoning models, such as OpenAI o1/o3 (OpenAI 2024) and DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI 2025), marks an explicit return to algorithmic search:

5.1 Decoupling Compute from Static Parameter Counts

In classical autoregressive inference, generating each token consumes a roughly fixed compute budget proportional to model parameter count (ignoring attention over the context):

\[\text{Compute}_{\text{step}} = \mathcal{O}(|\theta|)\]

By permitting models to execute variable-length reasoning traces, architectures trade test-time computational work for search depth. The system generates competing hypotheses, evaluates intermediate steps, and backtracks upon encountering formal contradictions.

5.2 Deterministic Verification vs Preference Approximation

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) optimises against a learnt neural reward model, which is susceptible to reward hacking and sycophancy. Conversely, contemporary reasoning frameworks employ deterministic ground-truth verifiers (code execution sandboxes, formal theorem provers, symbolic equivalence checkers). In domains where such verifiers exist, this mitigates reward hacking and aligns model optimisation directly with deductive consistency.

5.3 Approximating Algorithmic Induction

By using deep neural networks as proposal distributions and coupling them with search algorithms (such as Monte Carlo Tree Search) and deterministic verification, modern systems can be read as crude, resource-bounded approximations of the inductive search that Solomonoff induction idealises.

7 Concluding Synthesis

Intelligence is not the magnitude of parametric memory stored within static network weights. It is the computational efficiency with which an agent constructs, traverses, and formally verifies causal models across unfamiliar environment manifolds.

The frontier of artificial intelligence research is shifting decisively from passive token ingestion toward dynamic, verified computation. The ultimate measure of our architectures will not be the volume of historical tokens they can reproduce, but their capacity to discover minimal programs that master unencountered worlds.
